Neighboring cities Updated 2026-08-15

What Palo Alto's neighbors are doing about SB 79

Palo Alto isn't deciding SB 79 in isolation. Every Peninsula city with a qualifying transit station is making the same call — let SB 79 apply on July 1, 2026, or adopt a city ordinance first that exempts historic sites or rezones the transit parcels to 50% of SB 79 (the two fast off-ramps the law itself provides). This page tracks where the seven closest neighbors landed. As of today, Menlo Park is the most-watched outcome: a 5–1 Planning Commission vote against the exemption, then the City Council reaching the same answer on May 12 — no exemption, SB 79 applies in full.

What's an "off-ramp"? SB 79 itself (Gov. Code §65912.161(b)(1)) lets a city adopt an ordinance — effective until about 2032 — that pulls certain parcels out from under the state minimums. The statute lists six kinds of parcel that qualify, lettered (A) through (F). Two of them are the ones cities around here have actually used: (A) existing zoning already allows at least 50% of SB 79's density and FAR (so most of the upzone is already done locally), and (F) the parcel has a locally-listed historic resource. The other four are narrower: transit zones that already come close to SB 79's own density totals, parcels the state's opportunity maps mark as low-resource, parcels covered by a city's own transit-oriented development alternative plan, and land in a very high fire hazard zone or exposed to a foot of sea-level rise. (A) and (F) are the two Palo Alto staff recommended for June 1. See council watch for the Palo Alto detail.

City · Menlo Park · resolved

Menlo Park — Council declined the 50% exemption on May 12

Outcome: Menlo Park City Council declined to introduce staff's recommended temporary exemption ordinance on May 12, 2026. The Council reached consensus around "no action" rather than taking a formal vote — because staff's recommendation was an affirmative action (introduce the ordinance) that the Council collectively declined. Result: SB 79 applies in Menlo Park by default on July 1, 2026, with the state's standards (Tier 1: 75 ft / 120 du/ac / FAR 3.5 within ¼ mile; 95 ft / 160 du/ac / FAR 4.5 in the 200-ft adjacent band).

What staff proposed

Staff Report #26-078-CC recommended Option 1 — the 50% standards exemption under Gov. Code §65912.161(b)(1)(A). The ordinance would have created a new MPMC Chapter 16.57 excluding 130 parcels (43.3 acres) across five sub-districts of the El Camino Real/Downtown Specific Plan — D (Downtown), SA-W (Station Area West), SA-E (Station Area East), ECR-SE, and ECR-NE-R — from SB 79 until one year after the 7th-cycle Housing Element (no later than January 31, 2032). Staff's theory: existing zoning in those subdistricts already exceeds 50% of SB 79's density/FAR thresholds, so the off-ramp is available.

What the Planning Commission said

On April 27, 2026, the Planning Commission voted 5–1, Commissioner Ferrick dissenting, Commissioner Do absent, to not adopt the draft resolution and instead recommend Council take the fourth option — no action — and let SB 79 go into full effect. Quoting verbatim from the staff report:

"After its discussion, the Commission voted 5-1, with Commissioner Ferrick dissenting and Commissioner Do absent, to not adopt the draft resolution and instead recommended that the City Council not adopt the temporary exemption ordinance excluding eligible sites from SB 79 and let SB 79 go into full effect (effectively the fourth, No action, option). As part of its recommendation, the Planning Commission recommended the City Council direct staff to study the effect of SB 79 on the City at some point in the future."

— Staff Report #26-078-CC, p. H-3.5

The Commission was, in the staff report's own words, "generally supportive of the State's intention with SB 79." The single public commenter at the hearing was also opposed to the exemption, saying Menlo Park's existing zoning is too complicated and should be simplified.

What the Council said on May 12

Staff did not defer to the Commission — the May 12 staff report still recommended Council introduce the ordinance. The full meeting video is publicly archived (YouTube); the deliberation on Item H-3 starts around the 4-hour mark. After clarifying questions from Councilmember Combs about what "no action" actually means (answer: SB 79 applies at 100% on July 1), Council moved one by one to the same answer the Commission reached:

  • Mayor Nash: "I am inclined, as I sit here, for no action. And to let SB 79 go into effect."
  • Councilmember Taylor: "I will be supporting no action."
  • Councilmember Combs: "I would be in favor of taking no action here and letting SB 79 go into effect."
  • Councilmember Schmidt: initially expressed concern about a "dire" scenario without an exemption, then came around — "I'm really glad we're not closing this circle like it was feeling like so much… he put a label on the feeling like things were constricting, and it was starting to feel a little dire, and I think it's feeling a little less that way now."
  • Two further "on board with no action" / "supportive of no action" statements appear in the transcript without clear attribution — likely the fifth councilmember.

The Mayor asked, "Do we have a motion and a second on the table?" Staff or the City Attorney replied: "You don't need [a motion]. There's just no action. Okay, very good." That was the end of the item. The tentative May 19 second reading is moot — there is nothing to adopt.

What Council asked staff to do next

Mayor Nash asked staff to keep Council and the public apprised as SB 79 applications come in — "if we do need to regulate anything, or just… full transparency, so we know what's happening." That's a much lighter ask than the Planning Commission's recommendation (to study SB 79 and possibly bring back an exemption later). No timeline was set.

What's interesting about the Menlo Park outcome

  • Staff was overruled, twice. The Planning Commission rejected staff's recommendation 5–1 on April 27. The City Council reached the same answer on May 12 despite staff doubling down on the original recommendation in the second staff report. This isn't a city slow-walking into SB 79 because it had no choice — it's a city that had a worked-out off-ramp on the table and explicitly chose not to take it.
  • The historic question never became a separate fight. Staff confirmed during Q&A that SB 79 only applies to sites zoned residential, commercial, or mixed-use — so the two designated historic sites in the SB 79 footprint are already out of scope. Menlo Park didn't need a separate historic carve-out and didn't propose one. (This is different from Palo Alto, where the historic exemption ordinance is a substantive separate question because more historic resources are within the affected band.)
  • State Sen. Becker (D–Menlo Park) voted against SB 79. Despite the local senator's no vote, his city's council declined to use the off-ramps the bill provides for cities that want them.
  • Public comment skewed one direction. All three public speakers at the May 12 hearing opposed the exemption — June Shin (Housing Leadership Council of San Mateo County), Adina Levin, and Katherine Dumont each backed the Planning Commission's no-action recommendation, per the approved Council minutes. No commenter at the meeting supported staff's exemption.

Why Palo Alto residents should care

Menlo Park is the closest comparable Peninsula city making this decision on a faster clock than Palo Alto. Both have one Caltrain station and downtown commercial zoning in the affected band. Staff in both cities reached the same recommendation — use the 50% off-ramp. Menlo Park's elected officials said no; Palo Alto's June 1 vote will say yes or no on substantively the same question. If you're trying to predict what either body might do, the other city's deliberation is the closest available data point.

City · Atherton · compliance ordinance adopted

Atherton — adopted objective standards, not an off-ramp

Atherton has no rail station of its own, but the Menlo Park Caltrain station sits within a quarter mile of the town, so SB 79's Tier 1 band reaches a corner of Atherton — per the Town, seven properties on Glenwood Avenue, Laurel Street, and Victoria Drive.

What's happened

  • 2026-02-18: First reading before the City Council; continued to a second reading.
  • 2026-03-18: The City Council adopted (on its consent agenda, at second reading) zoning amendments adding Chapter 17.59 to the Atherton Municipal Code — "Regulations and Objective Standards for Development Projects sought Pursuant to Government Code Section 65912.157 (SB 79)." The Town's approved minutes record it as Ordinance 678, adopted as part of the consent calendar on a motion by Council member Lewis, seconded by Lane, 5–0 (ayes Lewis, Lane, Widmer, DeGolia, Holland; no noes, absences or abstentions). Staff submitted the adopted ordinance to the state on March 26.
  • The Town reports it has received notification from HCD that Atherton is in substantial compliance with SB 79.
  • The standards apply as of SB 79's July 1, 2026 effective date.
  • 2026-07-09 (reported, not yet verified): A Cupertino developer, AlphaX RE Capital, filed a preliminary application to replace the single-family house on the one-acre lot at 110 Glenwood Avenue — one of the seven affected parcels — with five three-story buildings totalling 30 townhomes, six of them below market rate (three at under 50% of area median income, three at 80–120%). Town officials describe the application as in the early stages of review. This is the first SB 79 project reported in Atherton.

Read

This is a different move from every other city on this page. Chapter 17.59 is a compliance ordinance — it writes objective standards for how SB 79 projects are processed under §65912.157, and the Town's own summary is explicit that it cannot set height below 75 feet, density below 120 homes per acre, or FAR below 3.5 on the affected parcels. It is not a §65912.161(b) off-ramp: nothing is exempted or reduced. Atherton — the Peninsula's lowest-density town — chose to regulate the processing details and let the state minimums stand.

The Glenwood Avenue proposal is the first test of that choice, and the notable thing about it is how small it is. Atherton's ordinance leaves the state floors intact — 75 feet, 120 homes per acre, 3.5 FAR. A project built to those numbers on a one-acre lot could be far larger than 30 townhomes at three storeys. The first filing asks for a fraction of what the law would allow, which is worth remembering when reading the claim that SB 79 forces towers onto quarter-acre suburbia. It is also only a preliminary application — a placeholder that freezes the standards in effect on its filing date. It is not an approval, and nothing has been before the Town Council.

City · Mountain View · historic exclusion proposed, not adopted

Mountain View — a historic-resource exclusion now, an alternative plan later

Mountain View's affected footprint is unusually large: downtown Mountain View Caltrain, San Antonio Caltrain (on the border with Palo Alto and Los Altos), plus four VTA light-rail stations (Whisman, Middlefield, Bayshore/NASA, Evelyn). Staff estimates SB 79 covers roughly 21% of city land area.

What's happened

  • 2025-12-09: Council voted 7–0 (motion Ramirez, second Hicks) to pause expansion of the historic register. The expansion had been pitched as a way to shield more downtown buildings from SB 79; Council declined to rush a blanket designation purely to beat the SB 79 clock.
  • 2026-01-27: Council directed staff to begin work on a Local Alternative Plan focused on protecting registered historic buildings near downtown Caltrain, and to develop objective development standards. To free staff capacity, Council deferred the Downtown Precise Plan update, Moffett Boulevard Precise Plan update, and the Dark Sky Ordinance. Staff stated the LAP will not be ready by SB 79's July 1, 2026 deadline regardless.
  • 2026-02-24: Council adopted its 2026 Legislative Platform, which reportedly includes an "SB 79 Alternative Plan Timelines" position. (Exact adopted language should be pulled from Legistar before being quoted here.)

What's proposed — not yet decided

  • 2026-08-19: The Environmental Planning Commission takes up a draft ordinance and zoning-map amendment that would exclude 24 historic properties from SB 79 (Item 5.1). The properties are the ones already on Mountain View's historic register as of January 1, 2025 that sit within a half mile of a qualifying transit stop — addresses on Castro, Villa, View, Hope, Loreto and Franklin streets, plus Bush, Calderon, Moffett, Oak, Palo Alto Ave, South Shoreline, Mountain View Ave and Wright. The city attorney's draft cites the clause of SB 79 that lets a city exclude locally registered historic sites by adopting its own ordinance first (Gov. Code §65912.161(b)(1)(F)), and the exclusion would last only until one year after the city adopts its next housing element — staff put that at roughly 2031. Nothing has been adopted. If the commission recommends it and Council agrees, staff's schedule is a second reading on October 13, 2026 and an effective date in November.
  • The same package builds a permanent route. Staff's report says a historic district "may serve as a basis to justify a permanent exemption from SB 79 within the district," and that the district process was drafted "to allow Council to initiate the designation of a historic district for that purpose." The 24-property exclusion is temporary; district designation would not be.

Read

Mountain View is running two tracks, and they are different mechanisms with different limits. The historic exclusion is the fast one: a city ordinance plus a zoning-map notation, no state approval needed, and it only reaches buildings that were already on the register before 2025 — which is exactly why the December 2025 fight over expanding that register mattered. The Local Alternative Plan is the slow one, closer to Palo Alto staff's Option D, and it is the only route that could shield resources added to the register later; staff said in January it would not be ready by July 1, and it still has not been adopted. In the meantime SB 79's state minimums have applied in Mountain View since July 1.

City · Sunnyvale · carve-out adopted (minutes final)

Sunnyvale — narrow Murphy Avenue historic carve-out

Sunnyvale's two affected stations — Sunnyvale Caltrain and Lawrence Caltrain — are both Tier 1 (over 72 weekday trains after electrification). The city is not pursuing a broad 50% off-ramp; the only active SB 79-specific ordinance in motion is a surgical heritage-district carve-out covering the historic block of South Murphy Avenue (PLNG-2026-0118).

What's happened

  • 2026-02-10: Council adopted its 2026 Legislative Platform with SB 79 discussion; no specific support/oppose position on SB 79 was reported from the meeting.
  • 2026-04-13: Planning Commission recommended the Murphy Station heritage-district carve-out ordinance (Legistar file 26-0354), which amends Zoning Code Ch. 19.96 to exclude the 100 block of South Murphy Avenue and surrounding heritage parcels from §65912.157. (The public hearing notice had listed a 4/27 PC hearing and a 6/2 Council first reading; the city's Legistar record shows everything ran earlier.)
  • 2026-05-05: City Council passed the first reading (Legistar file 26-0481, recorded as approved/passed).
  • 2026-05-19: Adopted as Ordinance No. 3253-26 — Item 1.L on the Council consent calendar (Legistar file 26-0518). Confirmed 2026-07-22: the meeting's official minutes are now final and record the consent-calendar motion covering Item 1.L carrying 7–0, all councilmembers present. With a 30-day effective date, the carve-out would be in force by ~June 18 — ahead of SB 79's July 1 start.

Read

Sunnyvale's mayor (Larry Klein) publicly flagged risks to mobile-home-park residents from SB 79 in late 2025 but hasn't taken a broader policy position against the law. The Murphy carve-out is a narrow defensive measure, not a city-wide off-ramp. Everywhere else in Sunnyvale's transit footprint, SB 79 has applied since July 1, 2026.

City · Redwood City · kept the state standards

Redwood City — chose the state standards on the record, and is writing its own plan next

Redwood City Caltrain is Tier 1. For most of the run-up the city looked like the clearest “no action” case on this page — no off-ramp ordinance, no historic carve-out, no tier determination. That changed on July 13, 2026, when the City Council took the question up directly.

What's happened

  • 2026-01-12: Council adopted its 2026 state legislative platform; no SB 79 position was reported.
  • 2026-07-13: On the Planning Commission's recommendation, the City Council voted unanimously to let SB 79's standards stand as-is rather than adopt an off-ramp, and directed staff to prepare a city TOD Alternative Plan — coordinated with the Greater Downtown Area Plan and regional policy, with added emphasis on community outreach. The plan is targeted for spring 2027. No ordinance number has surfaced: this was direction to staff, not an adopted ordinance.
  • The Greater Downtown Area Plan itself still targets adoption in late 2027.

Read

The practical outcome — SB 79 applies in full, with no city-side modifier — is the same one you'd get from doing nothing. But it isn't the same decision, and the distinction matters when reading this scorecard: Redwood City is the clearest Peninsula example of a council that looked at the off-ramp and declined it on the record, then started work on the one long-term tool SB 79 does allow. City staff also expect the near-term change to be modest, because the downtown already zones for more than SB 79 requires in places — Senior Planner Ellen Yau pointed to existing developments between the Caltrain station and El Camino Real that “already reach 80 feet tall and are between 180 and 215 dwelling units per acre,” against SB 79's 160 homes per acre within 200 feet of the station.

City · San Carlos · excluded its core sites

San Carlos — switched SB 79 off downtown, using the rule that rewards already-dense zoning

San Carlos Caltrain is Tier 1, and San Carlos has the most complete off-ramp of any city on this scorecard. Ordinance 1634 adds Chapter 18.25A to the municipal code and, "[p]ursuant to Government Code Section 65912.161(b)(1)," excludes every site that is both zoned MU-DC-100, MU-D-120, MU-SC-120 or RM-100 and located within one-quarter mile of the San Carlos Caltrain Station from SB 79's §65912.157 standards — "until one (1) year following the adoption of the seventh revision of the housing element." Those parcels "remain subject to applicable local standards." The path: Planning & Transportation Commission recommendation March 2, Council public hearing and introduction March 23, second reading on the April 13 consent calendar, effective 30 days later on May 13, 2026.

Read

This is the off-ramp that costs a city nothing, and San Carlos qualified for it precisely because its downtown was already zoned dense. §65912.161(b)(1)(A) lets a jurisdiction exclude sites that already permit at least 50% of SB 79's density and floor-area standards; staff's finding was simply that the four districts around the station clear that bar, and the ordinance "would not change existing MU-DC-100, MU-D-120, MU-SC-120, or RM-100 development standards." Note how different that is from the “50% off-ramp” this site tracks elsewhere: Palo Alto's and Menlo Park's staff proposals would have halved SB 79's standards on transit parcels, a reduction a council has to justify. San Carlos instead pointed at zoning it already had and removed the parcels from the statute. Both are 50 percent; only one requires giving anything up. Because San Carlos is under 35,000 people, its SB 79 footprint is the quarter-mile band alone — so an exclusion drawn at a quarter mile around the station reaches most of what the law would have touched. The follow-on is the same instrument Redwood City chose: staff say the temporary exclusion "will be replaced by a comprehensive SB 79-compliant Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) Alternative Plan developed in conjunction with the next housing element update."

City · Los Altos · likely below the population threshold

Los Altos — too small for SB 79's outer band

Los Altos has no rail station within its borders, and the city has produced no public SB 79 staff report or implementation ordinance. San Antonio Caltrain sits roughly 0.3 mile from the Los Altos border, so a half-mile radius from the station does reach across El Camino Real into north Los Altos. But SB 79's quarter-to-half-mile band only applies in a city of at least 35,000 people — Gov. Code §65912.157(a)(4) sets the Tier 1 outer-band floors (65 ft / 100 du/ac / FAR 3) only "within a city with a population of at least 35,000," and §65912.157(a)(6) does the same for Tier 2. Los Altos's population is 31,921 (California Department of Finance E-1, January 1, 2026), about 3,100 short. No part of Los Altos falls within a quarter mile of San Antonio Caltrain. So the state height and density floors this page describes elsewhere appear not to reach Los Altos at all. Los Altos has no rail station within its borders and has adopted no SB 79 implementation ordinance. It does have one city-commissioned document that mentions SB 79 — but it answers a different question than the one Los Altos residents need answered. A consultant report the Council received on June 23, 2026 states that “there are no major transit stops that are eligible for SB 79 … in Los Altos.” That is true and beside the point: SB 79 reaches into a city from stations outside it. San Antonio Caltrain sits roughly 0.3 mile from the Los Altos border, and a half-mile radius from the station reaches across El Camino Real into north Los Altos. The parcels inside that half-mile became SB 79–eligible on July 1, 2026, whether or not Los Altos has analyzed it — and on that question the city still has nothing on record.

What's happened

  • No SB 79-specific council action or implementation ordinance on file.
  • On June 23, 2026 the Council received an Elections Code §9212 impact report on the Measure D parking-plaza initiative, prepared by Metropolitan Planning Group and Bay Area Economics. Its housing-impacts section carries the city's only SB 79 paragraph. The Council received the report by unanimous roll call — it did not adopt its conclusions, and the minutes record no council discussion of SB 79 at all.
  • That paragraph also says Los Altos is “not currently included in” MTC's Transit Oriented Communities policy. That is a separate MTC instrument from the SB 79 Regional Map, which is what determines the tier and band; not being in one says nothing about the other.
  • The General Plan Update that launched January 14, 2026 references "new State laws" generically but does not name SB 79.
  • Los Altos Hills (a separate jurisdiction) discussed SB 79 at its May 15, 2025 council meeting; Los Altos itself did not.

Read

We previously called this the most concerning quiet on the scorecard — a slow-growth city with an unanalyzed band along El Camino Real east of San Antonio Road. That reading was wrong, and the reason is the population threshold above: the statute's outer-band standards simply never reach a city of 31,921. Los Altos's silence is most likely the correct answer to a question that doesn't apply to it, not a gap. The open question is narrower and worth stating plainly: SB 79's opening clause makes a qualifying project an allowed use "within one-half or one-quarter mile" of a stop, while the standards that preempt local height and density in the outer band are the ones carrying the 35,000 condition. Whether that leaves a sub-35,000 city's outer band entirely outside SB 79, or inside it as an allowed use at 30 du/ac with local standards still enforceable, is a question for the city attorney — not one this page should answer. The authoritative map is MTC's SB 79 Regional Map, which will become the official tier and band map on July 1, 2026. This is still the most concerning quiet on the scorecard, and the §9212 report makes it slightly worse rather than better. Los Altos's slow-growth political tradition makes it the city most likely to have wanted an off-ramp; what it has instead is a single sentence, written for a different purpose, that a reader could easily take as an all-clear. Residents in the affected band — mostly along El Camino Real east of San Antonio Road — still have no city document telling them SB 79 applies to their parcel, and now have one that appears to say it doesn't. What this tells us about Palo Alto's June 1 vote

Three patterns worth carrying into the June 1 discussion:

  1. The 50% off-ramp is unpopular even where staff recommend it. Menlo Park is the closest test case — staff proposed it, Planning Commission rejected it 5–1, Council rejected it again on May 12. Sunnyvale didn't propose one. Mountain View didn't propose one. Among Palo Alto's seven closest neighbors, the count of cities actively pursuing the 50% reduction on July 1 is now zero. The 50% figure that did get used is the other one: San Carlos's §65912.161(b)(1)(A) exclusion, which is available only to cities whose zoning already allows at least half of what SB 79 requires. Where a city's downtown is dense enough, the statute lets it opt out for free — and no council debate is needed to justify keeping the zoning you have.
  2. Historic carve-outs are pursued narrowly when at all. Sunnyvale's Murphy Avenue carve-out — now through both readings — is the only formal historic exemption ordinance to advance on the Peninsula outside Palo Alto. Mountain View paused its historic register expansion specifically to avoid the appearance of designating buildings as historic just to defeat SB 79. The political tolerance for broad historic exemptions appears low.
  3. Most cities are letting SB 79 apply by default and revisiting later. Los Altos — and now Menlo Park — have SB 79 in effect with no city-side modifier. Redwood City lands in the same place by the opposite route: its Council took the question up on July 13 and chose the state standards explicitly, with a TOD alternative plan now in progress (press-reported; not yet verified against the city record). Atherton lets the minimums stand too, adding only objective processing standards. Mountain View's LAP follows later. San Carlos is the exception: it excluded its core transit parcels outright under §65912.161(b). This is the de facto Peninsula posture, even where local elected officials have publicly criticized the law.

Palo Alto staff's June 1 proposal — the same 50% off-ramp Menlo Park staff proposed, paired with a historic exemption — is the most aggressive defensive package any of these eight cities is considering. The outcome of that vote will, by itself, change where Palo Alto sits on this scorecard.

Caveats and method

  • "Resolved," "in progress," and "silent" reflect formal city action visible in primary sources (staff reports, agendas, meeting videos), not predictions of what cities might do later.
  • Tier assignments use the post-electrification Caltrain schedule (all Peninsula Caltrain stations carry >72 weekday trains, qualifying as Tier 1) cross-referenced to the ABAG SB 79 Summary. The authoritative regional map — MTC's SB 79 Regional Map — becomes official July 1, 2026.
  • For Menlo Park's 5–1 vote and the May 12 Council outcome, primary sources are the staff report (verbatim recitation of the Commission resolution language), the official meeting video (transcribed via the Transcriber MCP tool), and — as of July 2026 — the approved minutes of both meetings (Planning Commission minutes approved June 8, 2026; Council minutes approved June 9, 2026), which corroborate the vote count and the "No action" record.
  • For the other five cities, primary-source confidence is lower in places where staff reports were not directly accessible. Specific 2026 council-meeting positions are documented where retrievable; where we relied on summaries or press coverage, that's noted in the per-city Sources list.
  • Los Altos specifically: the assertion that San Antonio's half-mile reaches into north Los Altos is geometric, not a confirmed city-issued GIS analysis. It is also, on its own, not enough to bring SB 79's standards to bear — §65912.157(a)(4) and (a)(6) condition the quarter-to-half-mile band on a city population of at least 35,000, and Los Altos is at 31,921 (DOF E-1, 2026-01-01). Confirm against the MTC map and the city's own population figure before relying on this for a specific parcel. The city's June 23, 2026 §9212 report does not settle it either way — it addresses stops located inside Los Altos, not bands projected in from outside.
